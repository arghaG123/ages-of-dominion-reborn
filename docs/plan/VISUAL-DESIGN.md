## 3. Shared visual language

### Materials, light and camera

Use a consistent high aerial three-quarter camera for the kingdom and its world objects. The reference's apparent angle, rather than an arbitrary label such as isometric, is the standard. Approximately 55–60 degrees above the ground is a practical initial calibration. Freeze a camera once the assembled comparison passes; record the final projection and object anchors. All world buildings, trees, walls, towers and actors must agree with that camera.

Daylight comes from upper-left with short soft shadows down-right. Grass is varied natural green, rivers reflect blue sky, stone is matte with visible courses, metal has restrained specular highlights, and the far mountains fade toward blue. Night or unusual biome lighting is a controlled variant, not an excuse to change every asset's rendering technique. Hero/item inspection may use a frontal camera and darker stage because it is a separate inspection view, not an actor pasted into the aerial world.

Do not use a fixed color-median gate to erase legitimate age/biome identity. Evaluate coherent light, contrast, camera and rendering finish together. Neither a correct average color nor a file with the correct dimensions can prove style acceptance.

### Interface

The world carries the visual weight. Open-world screens are full-bleed scenes with compact edge HUDs; details appear on deliberate selection. Do not fill the upper third with resource cards or turn Adventure into paragraphs stacked above a small framed map. The genre references show an environment first, controls second.

Use dark charcoal/blue stone surfaces with warm gilt/bronze rims. Gold accents mark active controls and important numbers; cyan is reserved for magic/selection effects, red for damage/danger, green for healing/production. Small symbols must read at actual phone size. Dark textured miniatures that disappear inside dark medallions are unsuitable navigation icons even if attractive at 512 pixels.

Space HUD controls on an 8px rhythm with 4px fine adjustments. This is an interface rule, not a requirement that natural world geography become a rigid spreadsheet. Caption fonts should remain readable at actual CSS size, with serif display titles and simple readable numerical labels. Do not stretch square ornamental frames into wide ribbons: use appropriate nine-slice borders or separately designed wide plates. Keep ornaments out of text bounds.

Visible secondary medallions may be around 32–40 CSS pixels and primary medallions 48–56. Their independent invisible hit targets must be at least 48×48, with no overlapping hit areas. Use clear short labels or a discoverable tooltip/accessibility name. A button must have focus, pressed, disabled and busy states appropriate to its action. Selection sheets also need empty/error states. Use restrained 150–250ms opacity/transform transitions, reduced-motion alternatives, and no perpetual decorative shaking.

### Persistent shell and responsive behavior

The primary viewport is 424×933 CSS pixels. Verify 360×800 and 820×1180 as well. Respect safe-area insets, the physical-device punch hole and gesture bar. Keep primary actions reachable without covering selected structures or turn targets. Landscape should reveal more lateral world space and reposition compact utilities; never rotate a tall page and leave its map below the viewport.

Portrait remains sufficient for every required action. Wider landscape is especially useful for Adventure and Tactical, but cannot be a prerequisite to playing those modes. Pan and bounded zoom provide access to the world; the default camera already shows the main kingdom composition clearly. A 16:9 reference cover-cropped into a 424×933 phone discards most of its width, so do not use cropping as the implementation strategy for landscape samples. Author registered world regions and camera bounds.
